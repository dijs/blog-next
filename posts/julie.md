Earlier this year, the world lost a wonderful person. For 16 years, she was an important part of my life. She was my mentor, my friend, and my mother-in-law.

Since this is a public blog, I'll leave out the more personal details and focus on something we shared: a love for engineering, learning, and helping others grow.

From the beginning of our relationship, she offered me guidance and encouragement. At nearly every stage of my career, she was someone I could turn to for advice, a thoughtful conversation, or an informed second opinion.

I remember discussing my very first interviews and job offers with her. Having spent decades working in engineering, across both large and small companies, she brought a wealth of experience and perspective that I was fortunate to learn from. She challenged me to think outside the box, question my assumptions, and continually push myself to improve.

She led by example.

Growing up in a small, traditional town, she forged her own path. Fiercely independent, strong-willed, and endlessly curious, she pursued an education, built a career, and never stopped learning. She refused to let her circumstances define what she could accomplish.

That curiosity was something I always admired.

I fondly remember the countless hours we spent working through code golf challenges together. I often came out ahead on the scoreboard, largely because I had more experience with programming languages and their syntax. But her ability to break down a problem, understand its underlying structure, and approach it from an unexpected angle was remarkable. She reminded me that knowing a programming language and knowing how to solve a problem are two very different skills.

Her curiosity extended far beyond engineering.

She had a gift for making friends wherever she went. She loved meeting people, discovering new cultures, and learning about the traditions that shaped them. One of her favorite questions to ask someone new was:

**"If your mother could make you anything to eat, what would it be?"**

It was such a simple question, but it revealed so much. It opened the door to conversations about family, culture, childhood, and the things that make us who we are. That was her way of connecting with people: through genuine curiosity and a desire to understand them.

Later in her career, that same curiosity and passion for helping others led her into teaching. She taught and lectured at schools across Texas, where she could put her remarkable ability to explain complex ideas, encourage students, and inspire the next generation of engineers to work.

At one point, she invited me to speak to her students about entering the engineering workforce. It was a meaningful experience for me. I had the opportunity to pass along some of the wisdom she had shared with me years earlier, combined with a few lessons I'd picked up along the way.

In many ways, that moment brought things full circle.

Today, as engineering enters a new era shaped by artificial intelligence, I often find myself thinking about her. I can easily imagine her right alongside us, experimenting with new tools, challenging assumptions, finding creative solutions, and pushing the boundaries of what's possible.

She would have loved having an entirely new set of problems to solve.

I miss her greatly.

When I think about the impact she had on my life, I realize that her greatest lessons weren't necessarily about engineering. They were about curiosity, perseverance, generosity, and the importance of investing in other people.

I can only hope to pass along the same love, wisdom, and encouragement that she so freely gave to me.
